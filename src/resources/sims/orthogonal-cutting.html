AE203 EP1: Manufacturing

Orthogonal cutting

The tool removes a layer of thickness to. The metal shears on one plane, the shear plane, and slides up the face of the tool as a chip. The layer stays whole until it reaches the shear plane. There it shears into thin slices, drawn as cards, as in Kalpakjian and Schmid, Fig. 21.4a. Each card slides over the one behind it, and the cards leave as separate pieces of the chip. Drag the view to turn it.

The shear angle follows from Merchant's result, φ = 45° + α/2 − β/2, with μ = tan β.

What to notice

  • Each card slides over the one behind it as it crosses the shear plane. This sliding is the shear strain.
  • The ends of the cards form steps on the back of the chip. This is why the back of a real chip is rough, while the side that slides on the tool is smooth and shiny (p. 642).
  • The chip is thicker than the layer that was cut, and it moves more slowly than the workpiece, because the volume of metal does not change.
  • A larger rake angle, or less friction on the tool face, gives a larger shear angle, a thinner chip and less shear strain, so less energy is needed.

Schematic; the card thickness is exaggerated, as in the book, and the small gaps between the cards in the chip are drawn only so that the separate cards can be seen. Equations 21.2 to 21.4 of Kalpakjian and Schmid (pp. 640–641): r = sin φ / cos(φ − α) and γ = cot φ + tan(φ − α).
